01 — THE CHALLENGE

Rethinking the role of AI in education.

As artificial intelligence becomes increasingly integrated into education, new opportunities are emerging for personalized learning, accessibility, and student support.

However, increased reliance on AI also raises questions about students' ability to think independently, develop social skills, and maintain meaningful connections with one another.

How might we integrate AI into education while preserving students' cognitive development, social skills, and human connection?
02 — THE INTERVENTION

A tool for thinking beyond the obvious.

The Thinking Box is a physical classroom tool designed to encourage students to explore different perspectives rather than relying solely on their initial interpretations or AI-generated responses.

As part of the design team, I contributed to developing the Thinking Box intervention and translating our future-focused concept into a tangible classroom experience. 05 — FEEDBACK & ITERATION

Making the experience more intuitive.

Following our facilitation practice round, we identified opportunities to improve the clarity, usability, and overall flow of the workshop. Feedback highlighted that participants needed a clearer understanding of how the Thinking Box would work, more space to visualize their ideas, and a more intuitive way to reflect on the intervention's impact.

Original approach Feedback / opportunity Iteration
Verbal explanation of the intervention. The process was difficult to follow. Created step-by-step instructional slides.
Sticky notes for sketching. Limited space for visualizing ideas. Added larger sheets of paper.
Abrupt workshop ending. The closing needed more structure. Added a reflection and takeaway activity.
Using the box for both prompts and evaluation. The interaction felt confusing. Introduced a separate four-section reflection matrix.
06 — INDIVIDUAL DESIGN DEVELOPMENT

Translating prompts into meaningful discussion.

I further developed the classroom interaction to demonstrate how the Thinking Box could be used during an actual learning activity. Using a discussion of The Great Gatsby as an example, the process illustrates how students can use the perspective coins to challenge their interpretations and build on one another's ideas.

01

Introduce a discussion

The teacher introduces a discussion question about the book.

Why does Gatsby go to such great lengths to be with Daisy?
02

Select a perspective coin

Students randomly select a coin from the Thinking Box. Each coin introduces a different way to approach the discussion.

WHY

Why?

Why does Gatsby pursue Daisy?

?

Question

Is Gatsby in love with Daisy or his idealized version of her?

POV

Point of view

How might Daisy interpret Gatsby's actions differently?

ACCESS

Accessibility and life experiences

How might different life experiences and opportunities influence how someone interprets Gatsby's pursuit of Daisy?

03

Discuss and reconsider

Students share their interpretations, challenge assumptions, and consider whether the additional perspective changes their understanding.

04

Flip and pass

Students flip the coin and pass it to the next student. The next student continues the discussion using the same perspective, allowing multiple voices to contribute.

07 — EVALUATING THE INTERVENTION

Understanding the impact of a new perspective.

We separated the reflection activity from the Thinking Box itself. Participants now place their coin in a four-section matrix to communicate how much the prompt influenced their original idea.

Significant change

The coin substantially changed our original idea.

Some change

The coin expanded or adjusted our idea.

Minor change

The coin introduced a small consideration.

No change

The coin did not significantly alter our idea.

08 — REFLECTION & NEXT STEPS

Designing for connection, not replacement.

This project reinforced the importance of translating speculative futures into tangible, understandable experiences. Through the facilitation practice and feedback process, I learned that an intervention must be intuitive enough for participants to understand without extensive explanation.

I also learned that physical design can serve as more than a delivery mechanism for information. In this case, the coins act as conversation starters that encourage students to question assumptions, listen to others, and develop ideas collaboratively.

Moving forward, the Thinking Box could be tested with actual students and educators to evaluate how different prompts influence participation, discussion, and independent thinking in classroom environments.
